Objective

The goal of this project was to design and build a functional radio-controlled vehicle capable of wireless operation, copper-plate detection, ultrasonic distance sensing, and movement over a 20° incline. The project required the integration of mechanical, electrical, and embedded systems into a single working vehicle.

The design also had several constraints: the final vehicle had to include at least one custom PCB and a critical 3D-printed component, remain within a $120 CAD component budget, and could not use Arduino, Arduino IDE, or pre-built modules beyond those specifically permitted. The vehicle also needed to use a team-designed wireless controller and display real-time distance measurements.

Overview

The project combined embedded programming, custom PCB design, power management, wireless communication, metal detection, distance sensing, and mechanical design. The final vehicle used two ESP32 microcontrollers, one for the car and one for the remote controller. The car used four DC gear motors for all-wheel drive, an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor for distance measurement, and a custom contact-based system for detecting copper plates. The controller used a dual-axis joystick for driving, push buttons for additional functions, and an LCD to display distance measurements. Two custom PCBs were designed: a power board for battery protection, voltage regulation, and motor control, and a remote-control PCB for the ESP32, joystick, buttons, and LCD interface. The mechanical system was designed in Fusion 360 and iterated alongside the electrical and software systems.

The drivetrain went through several major iterations as testing revealed limitations in torque, weight, and reliability. The initial design used rear-wheel drive with external gearboxes. Testing showed that the motors could spin freely but lacked sufficient torque when the vehicle was under load. Several gearbox designs were then tested, including a fully 3D-printed gearbox, plastic gears, and metal gears. Plastic gears experienced rapid wear, while metal gears increased the vehicle's weight without sufficiently improving performance. The final design switched to four 12 V high-torque DC gear motors, creating an all-wheel-drive system without external gearboxes. This reduced mechanical losses, simplified the drivetrain, and provided enough torque for the vehicle to successfully climb the ramp.

Metal detection was one of the most heavily iterated subsystems. The project began with an NE555 timer-based induction circuit, followed by a transistor oscillator and then a multi-stage induction balance circuit using a 7414 Schmitt trigger, TL072 op-amp, envelope detector, and LM339 comparator. Although these circuits demonstrated metal-detection principles during bench testing, they became unreliable when integrated into the moving vehicle because of vibration and electromagnetic interference from the motors. A fourth iteration used a Raspberry Pi Pico with ADC-based digital pulse induction and automated baseline calibration. While this improved detection, motor-generated electrical noise still caused false positives. The final solution therefore pivoted to a contact-based detection system. Two probes attached to the vehicle contacted the copper plate and completed an electrical circuit, triggering a buzzer. A custom 3D-printed mechanism allowed the probes to move when climbing the ramp and return to position afterward.

An HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor was mounted at the front of the vehicle to measure distances to obstacles. Because the sensor operates at 5 V while the ESP32 uses 3.3 V logic, a voltage divider was implemented on the Echo signal. During testing, incorrect resistor values on the PCB caused unstable readings. The resistors were identified and replaced, restoring reliable distance measurements.

Two custom PCBs were designed in KiCad. The power PCB handled the 12 V battery supply, fuse protection, MOSFET protection, voltage regulation, decoupling, and motor-driver circuitry. High-current paths required wide traces and careful grounding to manage heat, voltage drops, and electrical noise. The remote-control PCB integrated the ESP32, joystick, push buttons, and LCD. Dense GPIO routing required careful trace placement and consideration of signal integrity. Several PCB issues were discovered during testing, including an incorrect use of ESP32 GPIO2, which is subject to boot-strapping constraints, and problems with the LCD level-shifting circuit. Rather than completely redesigning the boards, the issues were resolved through external rewiring, circuit modifications, and breakout boards.

The embedded software was developed from scratch using ESP-IDF and Visual Studio Code. The controller reads joystick positions through the ESP32's ADC, processes the inputs into movement commands, and transmits them wirelessly using ESP-NOW. The car receives these commands and controls four motors while simultaneously measuring distance with the HC-SR04. The controller also receives distance data from the car and displays it on the LCD. The software was organized into separate modules for joystick input, ESP-NOW communication, LCD control, motor control, and ultrasonic sensing, making the system easier to debug and modify.

Objective

The goal was to design a complete PCB that could act as the electrical interface between an RC car's power source, microcontroller, and motors. The board was designed to integrate power distribution, regulated logic power, motor driving, and protection circuitry while remaining practical to manufacture.

The design was required to include microcontroller interfacing for control signals, a battery input, a regulated 5 V supply, a motor-driver circuit, at least one protection feature, status LEDs, test points, and mounting holes. To maintain compatibility with low-cost PCB manufacturing, the design was limited to a maximum 10 cm × 10 cm board size and two copper layers. Power traces also had to be appropriately sized for the expected motor current.

Overview

The PCB was designed to act as the connection between the RC car's power source, microcontroller, and motors. The design was divided into several functional sections: power input and protection, voltage regulation, motor control, status indication, and testing. The first step was creating the schematic and identifying the components required for each section. The final component selection included the DRV8251ADDAR motor driver, AP63205WU-7 voltage regulator, FDS4435BZ MOSFET, fuse, protection diodes, capacitors, resistors, LEDs, connectors, and test points.

The power section was designed first because it determines how power is distributed throughout the rest of the board. The input path includes F1, a 0685T5000-01 fuse, providing overcurrent protection. Z2 (SMBJ15CA-Q) was included as a protection device across the supply, while Z1 (MMSZ5231B-7-F) provides additional voltage protection within the circuit. The FDS4435BZ MOSFET was also incorporated into the power circuitry. The basic power relationships were considered when designing the distribution network: P = VI. This relationship is important because the motor section can require substantially more current than the logic circuitry. This influenced the separation of the higher-current motor/power paths from the lower-current control circuitry.

The logic supply was designed around IC1, the AP63205WU-7, with L1 (4.7 µH) forming part of the regulator circuit. The surrounding capacitors were selected to support the power-regulation and filtering portions of the circuit. The BOM includes multiple 0.1 µF capacitors as well as larger-value capacitors, allowing different parts of the circuit to have appropriate local filtering and energy storage. The same power relationship seen before helps determine the current that must be carried through the input and regulated-power portions of the PCB.

The main motor-control section uses the DRV8251ADDAR. The motor driver receives control signals from the microcontroller through J*, while the motor connections are brought out through the board connectors. The driver circuitry was laid out so that the motor/power paths could be kept physically distinct from the lower-current control connections. This is important when routing a compact two-layer board because the motor paths carry the larger currents and therefore require greater attention to trace width and routing. The PCB outline required the design to remain within 10 cm × 10 cm and use two copper layers, so routing had to balance electrical requirements with the available board area.

After completing the schematic, the components were transferred into the PCB layout. Placement was organized according to function rather than positioning components randomly. The power-related components were kept together, with the regulator and its associated components grouped into the power section. The motor driver and motor connections were positioned to create a practical high-current path, while the control interface and indicators were placed where they could be accessed more easily. The board also included five test points (TP1–TP5). These provide locations where important electrical nodes can be probed during testing without having to directly contact small component pads.

One of the most important layout decisions was determining how wide the PCB traces needed to be. For a conductor, the approximate resistance can be expressed as: R = ρ × L / A, where:(R) = trace resistance, (ρ) = copper resistivity, (L) = trace length, (A) = cross-sectional area. As trace width increases, the cross-sectional area increases, reducing resistance. Lower resistance also reduces the voltage lost across the trace: V_drop = I × R and the power dissipated in the trace is: P_loss = I² × R. This makes wider traces particularly important for the motor and main power paths. The power traces are sized according to the motor's current requirements and recommended by the Saturn PCB Toolkit for determining trace widths based on current and temperature rise.

Once component placement was established, the connections were routed across the two copper layers. The routing process involved balancing several requirements:

-Keeping high-current power and motor traces sufficiently wide

-Providing connections between components without unnecessary detours

-Separating different functional sections where practical

-Maintaining connections between the microcontroller and motor-control circuitry

-Working within the board's physical dimensions(leaving room for mounting holes, connectors, and test points)

Using two layers meant that traces could be distributed between the top and bottom copper layers instead of routing everything on a single surface. After routing, the design was reviewed in the 2D PCB view to inspect the top and bottom traces. The board was then viewed in 3D to check the physical arrangement of components and confirm that the PCB represented a practical physical assembly.

Objective

The goal of this project was to design and build an autonomous robot capable of navigating an 8 ft × 8 ft course, retrieving magnetic payloads, and delivering them to a designated drop-off area within a 10-minute time limit. The robot needed to follow a tape-guided route, detect and avoid obstacles, identify magnetic payloads, and transport them without human control.

The project was subject to strict material restrictions. The robot had to use the provided electrical components and 3D-printed components, with no new purchases allowed. This required the team to design around the available sensors, motors, and microcontroller while keeping the mechanical design compact and reliable.

Overview

We first brainstormed several payload retrieval concepts, including a magnetic rod, pinchers, claw, “patty flipper,” and forklift. Each concept was compared based on mechanical complexity, weight distribution, reliability, required motor and sensor usage, and how easily it could be integrated with the navigation system. The rear-facing forklift was selected because it provided a relatively simple lifting mechanism while keeping the front of the robot clear for navigation and obstacle-detection sensors.

After selecting the forklift concept, we developed the physical layout around the required sensors and mechanisms. Three ultrasonic sensors were positioned around the front and sides of the robot to provide distance measurements and reduce blind spots during navigation. An infrared line sensor was used to detect the black course tape, while an IR photodiode was used to identify the drop-off area. The MPU-6050 gyroscope provided orientation feedback so the robot could track its angular position after turns and obstacle-avoidance maneuvers. A reed switch was incorporated near the payload mechanism to identify the magnetic payload, while a limit switch provided confirmation that the payload had reached the correct position on the forklift. This allowed the robot to distinguish between a normal obstacle and a target payload rather than treating every detected object the same way.

The mechanical design was then translated into a physical prototype using 3D-printed components. The main casing, sensor mounts, and rear forklift were designed to fit the available motors, sensors, and electronics while keeping the robot compact enough to navigate the course. During assembly, some of the 3D-printed components had tighter tolerances than expected, particularly around the forklift and guide rails. Rather than completely redesigning the parts, we manually adjusted the clearances using sandpaper until the mechanisms could move smoothly while maintaining their alignment. This iterative approach allowed the printed components to be adapted to the actual fit of the assembled hardware.

The software was developed alongside the physical system using a modular approach in MicroPython. Instead of writing one large autonomous routine, the navigation system was divided into smaller functions that could be independently tested and then combined into larger behaviours. Functions were developed for motor control, ultrasonic distance averaging, line detection, reed-switch detection, IMU calibration, angular-position tracking, forklift movement, tape searching, tape realignment, and obstacle avoidance. For example, multiple ultrasonic readings were averaged to reduce unreliable individual measurements, while the gyroscope was calibrated at startup to establish a reference for angular tracking. This modular structure made it easier to isolate problems, adjust individual behaviours, and integrate the completed functions into the main autonomous routine.

The navigation logic was built around a sequence of sensor-driven decisions. At startup, the robot searched for the black tape and aligned itself with the course. During normal movement, the line sensor provided course guidance while the ultrasonic sensors continuously checked for obstacles. If an obstacle was detected, the robot stopped, compared the available space on either side, performed a 90° avoidance turn, moved around the obstruction, and searched for the tape again. The recorded gyroscope heading was then used to realign the robot with its original route. When an object was encountered near the payload area, the reed switch was used to determine whether it was magnetic; if identified as a payload, the robot approached it and activated the forklift. This created a layered control system where individual sensors handled specific tasks while the main routine coordinated their outputs.

Testing was performed progressively rather than waiting until the entire robot was assembled. Individual sensors and mechanisms were checked first, followed by motor movement, forklift operation, obstacle detection, and navigation behaviours. Sensor thresholds and sensitivities were adjusted based on observed readings, and the gyroscope was calibrated to reduce drift. Wiring was also reorganized to improve the use of the limited internal space and reduce the possibility of shorts. Once individual functions were reliable, they were combined into the larger autonomous routines for payload navigation and drop-off. This step-by-step process made it possible to identify whether an issue originated from the mechanical system, electronics, sensor readings, or software rather than debugging the entire system simultaneously.

A major part of the development process involved troubleshooting hardware failures. Several Raspberry Pi Pico boards failed during testing, requiring the team to repeatedly rewire the system, reload the program, and isolate the source of the failure. The reed switch was also initially identified as faulty after troubleshooting and consultation with multiple TAs. Rather than redesigning the entire system around the issue, the team isolated the faulty component and replaced it, allowing the intended magnetic-detection system to operate. These setbacks required repeated testing and adaptation but also provided practical experience in diagnosing hardware faults and separating component failures from problems in the design itself. The final system was able to operate as intended once the faulty component was replaced.

Objective

The objective of this project was to design and build a portable indoor garden capable of automatically monitoring and maintaining growing conditions for a plant. The system was designed to use a Raspberry Pi Pico to monitor soil moisture, soil temperature, and ambient light, then respond automatically by controlling a water pump and LED grow light. The design also needed to address practical concerns such as water management, drainage, flooding prevention, electrical safety, waterproofing, ease of maintenance, and efficient use of water and energy.

A major design consideration was ensuring that the electronics remained protected while still allowing the sensors and actuators to operate effectively. The system therefore required integration of mechanical, electrical, and software components into a single reliable system rather than treating each subsystem independently.

Overview

The design process began with brainstorming and evaluating several possible garden configurations. Concepts included a tube garden, beam box, cross-box garden, string watering system, water-spout design, and tower design. Each concept was compared based on water distribution, pump strain, drainage, flooding risk, electrical safety, material usage, portability, lighting coverage, plant growth, and ease of maintenance. This evaluation helped identify problems with several of the initial concepts before committing resources to fabrication.

For example, the tube and tower concepts provided compact layouts but required water to be pumped vertically, increasing the demand on the pump and creating additional concerns about how to protect the electronics. The beam box provided broad water and light distribution but introduced risks from having an elevated water system near electrical components. Other designs provided good drainage but were either bulky or difficult to integrate with the lighting system. Evaluating these tradeoffs helped narrow the design toward a solution that balanced mechanical simplicity, water efficiency, and electrical safety.

The final design used a bottom-feeder watering system. Instead of spraying water onto the plant from above, water was supplied from below the soil, allowing the soil to absorb water as needed. This approach reduced concerns associated with excessive top-down watering and provided better control over moisture while reducing unnecessary water waste. It also allowed the pump to operate with less vertical lifting than several of the alternative designs, reducing pump strain.

The physical structure was designed in Fusion 360, where custom components and the overall garden assembly were developed before fabrication. The design was made modular so that dimensions could be adjusted for different plant sizes. Components were then manufactured using 3D printing, allowing the physical design to be quickly tested and modified. This iterative approach made it possible to identify mechanical issues such as component placement, tubing routing, and accessibility before finalizing the assembly.

Electrical safety was incorporated into the mechanical design from the beginning. Separate compartments were included for the electronics so that the Raspberry Pi Pico and other electrical components could remain isolated from the water system. Tubing was routed underneath the circuitry to keep water paths away from sensitive electronics. A protective lid was also incorporated to cover internal components and provide a cleaner, more maintainable final assembly.The electrical system was developed alongside the mechanical design. The Raspberry Pi Pico was connected to a STEMMA soil sensor through I²C to obtain soil moisture and temperature measurements. A photoresistor connected to an ADC input was used to measure ambient light, while digital outputs controlled the water pump and LED grow light. A float switch was incorporated as an additional safety mechanism to prevent the pump from operating when the water level was already high.

I implemented the watering logic using moisture thresholds, timed watering intervals, and the float switch as a safety condition to prevent flooding. I also added sensor error handling and reinitialization for reliable operation. I contributed to the electrical and mechanical design of the automated indoor garden by evaluating the garden design, with a focus on water management, electrical safety, and component placement. During testing, I helped troubleshoot sensor communication, water flow, and control behavior, making adjustments to the hardware layout and software thresholds to improve the reliability of the final prototype.
